Does copying smart money on prediction markets actually pay?

A research bot watches profitable Polymarket wallets, rejects almost everything they do, and publishes what survives. Every published ticket is then opened as a paper position at the price shown, committed to a public repository the moment it opens, and held to the venue's own settlement. This page is the write-up: the method, the three accounting errors the record caught, and what the trial concluded.

Bruce McGinley — GitHub LinkedIn brucepmcginley@gmail.com

Result

—

The honest reading: no edge was found. The picks win at almost exactly the rate their entry price implied — the calibration gap is a fraction of a percentage point — which is what you would expect from buying at the market's own number. Trading fees then take the rest. The strategy is not losing badly; it is failing to beat the price it pays.

The sample is far too small to prove failure — the bootstrap interval on the mean per-bet return still straddles zero in both directions. What can be said is stronger than the P&L suggests: all three accounting errors found during the trial pointed the same way, and every one of them had been making the result look better than it was. A process that only ever errs in its own favour is worth distrusting before its numbers are.

How it works

01
Watch proven wallets. Polymarket's public leaderboards across all-time, monthly and weekly windows define who counts as smart money. A wallet qualifies on realized profit and an ROI floor, which excludes churny market-makers who move size without making money. Their large buys come off the public trade feed.
02
Throw most of it away. Twelve filters reject in-play markets, anything resolving too soon or too far out, stale signals, prices that already ran past the whale's entry, and any second bet on an event already held. Most days nothing survives, and that is the intended outcome.
03
Rank and size what's left. Conviction blends wallet agreement, size behind the trade, freshness, price drift and time to resolution. Position size respects fixed house caps against current capital.
04
Record it, don't trade it. There is no execution path. Every surviving ticket is written into the paper trial instead, priced at the ask and charged the venue's taker fee.

Sources

Polymarket leaderboards. Top wallets by realized PnL in four categories, over three time windows.

Polymarket large-trade feed. Recent taker buys above a dollar floor, kept only when the buyer is one of those wallets. This is the single source that produces tickets today.

Kalshi is built and runnable as an independent price on the same events, and deliberately does not feed the board. Matching markets by title produced confident false edges — Kalshi's "who will run for the nomination" against Polymarket's "will X win it" is a 48¢ gap that is simply correct. After guarding against those, genuine disagreements among liquid markets came to zero.

Three further sources — logical-consistency arbitrage, a crypto risk-neutral density priced off Deribit's option chain, and CPI brackets against the Cleveland Fed nowcast — run in research only. They have no settled record, and a source with no record does not get to size a position.

A weighted blend, deliberately simple enough to check by hand. It ranks ideas against each other; it is not a probability of winning. Most live signals come from a single wallet, which caps the first term at a third of its weight, so a lone-wallet ticket rarely scores above about 0.80.

A bar, not a quota. The board used to publish the day's top eight. That answers "how many fit on the page", which is not a fact about any trade: it bought the eighth-best idea on a quiet day and skipped a better one on a busy day. Now every ticket above the conviction bar is published and none below it is, however many that turns out to be.

Where the bar sits is a guess, and it is meant to stop being one. Nothing in the blend was fitted to a result — the weights were chosen, not learned. So tickets in a band below the bar are recorded by the trial without being recommended. Watching only the trades you would have taken tells you nothing about the ones you skipped, and a cutoff you never observe the far side of can never be shown to be wrong.

Sizing

House limits, applied against current account value: every contract must cost at least $0.01, with a cap of 19 contracts per market and 38 per event, per $1,000 of account. On top of that the board risks 1% of the account per idea, scaled by conviction. Positions were a flat $100 until 16 August 2026 and are sized by these rules after it, so per-position returns are the comparable figure across that line — not dollars.

How the record is kept

The point of the trial is not the P&L. It is that the P&L cannot be edited afterwards.

Append-only. A position is committed to the public repository at the moment it opens, with its entry price and timestamp, and is never rewritten. The settlement label comes from the venue's own resolution — no hand entry, no discretion, no quietly deleting the bad ones. Anyone can check any row against the commit that created it.

Corrections happen at score time, not on the rows. When the accounting turns out to have been wrong, the rows stay exactly as they were written and the scoring function is fixed, so the headline changes and the history does not. Each such change is stamped as a dated change point, and the record page can be sliced before or since any of them.

Blocked signals are recorded too. Every refusal is logged with its reason, so an empty day is explained rather than mysterious, and so the rejection rules themselves can be audited after the fact.

Corrections

Three accounting errors surfaced during the trial. Every one of them had been flattering the result — which is the most useful thing the record produced.

What was wrongWhy it flatteredEffect on the headline
Maker fees on taker fills Every entry is priced at the ask, so it pays the taker fee. 287 settled rows had been booked at the maker rate — a quarter of taker. Mean return per bet fell from +3.1% to +1.3%.
The fee was outside the bar The copy sleeve budgeted for price drift past the whale's entry but not for the fee it also paid — money given up just as irreversibly, and largest exactly at mid-book. Fewer tickets clear; a mid-priced market now gets less room to drift than a lopsided one.
Mutually exclusive legs scored apart The trial refused a second bet on a market it held, but not on the same event. It booked YES on a high of 87–88° and YES on 89–90°, same city, same day, as two independent opinions. Anti-correlated legs shrink measured variance and inflate the count of independent draws. Opinions collapsed by roughly a fifth; mean return per bet fell from −0.88% to −2.87%. No money moved — total P&L was identical.

Figures are the headline as it stood when each correction landed. The record can be sliced at every one of these dates.

The gate

Nothing goes live on a good-looking chart. The same deploy gate the backtester applies to any other strategy in this project has to pass three things at once: a deflated Sharpe ratio of at least 0.95, a positive mean per-bet return, and a block-bootstrap 5% confidence interval on that mean that sits above zero.

The deflated Sharpe ratio is the load-bearing one. It penalises a result for how many variants were tried before it appeared, which is the honest answer to a backtest that looks good on the eleventh attempt. It also assumes independent draws — which is precisely why the third correction above mattered more than its P&L impact suggested.

—

Why it rejects so much

On 26 June an earlier, automated version placed four live orders. All four were on football matches already in their second half, and two of them were the same opinion twice — YES on one team and NO on the other, in both games.

Whales react to a goal faster than any polling loop, so those fills came at the post-event price with the information already gone. The in-play filter, the resolution window, the drift ceiling and the one-ticket-per-event rule all exist because of that afternoon. Replaying those four signals through today's filters produces nothing.

That last rule stayed too narrow for a long time, in the way described under Corrections above. It now refuses any leg of an event already held, and the record scores the ones already on file as one opinion apiece.

What this is not

It is not a bot, and it has no execution path — the code that once placed orders now refuses to construct, and going live again would be a separate decision behind its own deploy gate. It is not financial advice, and the conviction score is uncalibrated. Bankroll here is small enough that this phase is about proving process, not income.
